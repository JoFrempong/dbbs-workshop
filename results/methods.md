# Methods

## Data source

We analysed the subset of the Moving Pictures of the Human Microbiome study (Caporaso et al., 2011) distributed with the QIIME 2 "Moving Pictures" tutorial (raw data: ENA study ERP021896, BioProject PRJEB19825, Qiita study 550). The data comprise 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of 34 samples from two adults (subject-1 and subject-2), collected from four body sites (gut [stool], left palm, right palm and tongue) at up to five visits on days 0, 84, 112, 140 and 168 of the study (28 October 2008 to 14 April 2009). The design was unbalanced: subject-2 had no samples at day 112, and subject-1 had no left palm sample at day 0 and no gut sample at day 168, giving 8 gut, 8 left palm, 9 right palm and 9 tongue samples (`results/01_qc_design.csv`). Antibiotic use was reported for all seven day-0 samples and for no later sample, so antibiotic exposure is completely confounded with the first visit and could not be analysed. Upstream processing had been done in QIIME 2 (version 2026.7.0) by the tutorial authors. The details below are taken from the provenance records inside the published artifacts (`original/table.qza`, `original/taxonomy.qza`).
- **Demultiplexing:** single-end, barcoded reads in Earth Microbiome Project format were demultiplexed (`demux emp_single`, Golay barcode error correction).
- **Denoising:** DADA2 (`dada2 denoise_single`) produced 770 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Settings: reads truncated at 120 bases, no left trimming, maximum expected errors 2.0, truncation quality 2, independent sample pooling, consensus chimera removal (minimum fold parent over abundance 1.0), and up to 10⁶ reads used to learn error rates. ASVs are identified by MD5 hashes of their sequences.
- **Taxonomy:** each ASV was assigned with a naive Bayes classifier (`feature-classifier classify_sklearn`, scikit-learn 1.7.1; confidence threshold 0.7). The classifier had been trained (`fit_classifier_naive_bayes`; 7-mer features, α = 0.001) on full-length Greengenes 13_8 sequences clustered at 85% identity (`85_otus.fasta`, `85_otu_taxonomy.txt`). The reference was not trimmed to the amplified region.

Sample collection, DNA extraction, amplification of the 16S rRNA gene and sequencing were performed as described in the original study (Caporaso et al., 2011). We used the tab-separated count, taxonomy and sample-metadata tables derived from these artifacts. The count table was checked against `table.qza` itself, as described under Data processing.

## Data processing

All analyses were run in R from the raw tables, one script per step (`scripts/01_qc.R` to `scripts/07_counts_from_qza.R`).

To confirm that the tab-separated count table faithfully reproduces the published artifact, we rebuilt the table directly from `table.qza` without QIIME 2 (`scripts/07_counts_from_qza.R`).
- The archive (a zip file) was extracted to a temporary directory.
- `data/feature-table.biom` was verified against the archive's SHA-512 checksum.
- The BIOM 2.1 (HDF5) file was read with rhdf5. The sparse matrix (2,327 non-zero entries; 770 ASVs × 34 samples) was reconstructed from both its observation-wise (compressed sparse row) and sample-wise (compressed sparse column) copies, which were confirmed identical.
- The rebuilt table matched `counts.tsv` exactly: the same ASV and sample identifiers, the same order, 153,807 reads in total, and no differing cells.

Sample and ASV identifiers were checked for agreement and duplicates across the three tables. ASVs classified as chloroplast (class "Chloroplast"; 12 ASVs) or mitochondria (family "mitochondria"; 18 ASVs) were removed as non-bacterial, leaving 740 ASVs; these accounted for 2,283 of 153,807 reads and up to 14.3% of reads in a single sample. After removal, sequencing depth ranged from 830 to 9,770 reads per sample (median 3,927). Depth was unevenly distributed: all five subject-1 right palm samples were among the six shallowest (830–1,125 reads), whereas subject-2's right palm and tongue samples from day 84 onward were 3–8 times deeper than their day-0 samples. These deeper samples are exactly those with sample-ID prefixes L4 and L6, which may indicate a separate sequencing run; the metadata contain no run information, so this could not be confirmed. For diversity analyses, each sample was rarefied without replacement to the depth of the shallowest sample (830 reads), so all 34 samples were retained; rarefaction was repeated 100 times (random seed 42) and diversity values or distance matrices were averaged across draws. Rarefaction curves and the slope of each curve at 830 reads (`vegan::rareslope`) were used to assess whether richness had levelled off at that depth. Because DADA2 had removed singleton ASVs, coverage estimators that rely on singletons were not used. Relative abundance for taxonomic summaries was calculated on unrarefied counts as each taxon's share of the sample's reads after organelle removal. ASVs without a genus assignment were labelled by their deepest assigned rank (e.g. "Unassigned (f. Lachnospiraceae)"); such labels pool several unnamed genera. Greengenes 13_8 names were retained (e.g. Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes). One ASV labelled *Gallibacterium* (Pasteurellaceae), a genus associated with poultry, was reported as "Pasteurellaceae (GG: Gallibacterium)" because it is likely a misassigned oral Pasteurellaceae such as *Haemophilus*; sequences were not available to verify this.

## Statistical analysis

**Alpha diversity.** For each rarefied draw, we calculated observed ASVs and the Shannon index (natural logarithm), and averaged each over the 100 draws (`scripts/02_alpha_diversity.R`). The Shannon index was the primary metric because it is less sensitive to sequencing depth; observed ASVs is reported as richness at 830 reads. Alpha diversity was summarised by median and range per body site and subject. Body sites were compared with a blocked permutation test that respects the repeated-measures design. The test statistic was the F statistic for body site in the linear model diversity ~ visit + body site, where visit (subject × day; 9 visits) is a fixed block. Site labels were permuted only among samples from the same person on the same day (9,999 permutations; `permute::shuffleSet`), and P was calculated as (1 + number of permuted F ≥ observed F) / (number of permutations + 1). This tests the null hypothesis that body site makes no difference to diversity within a person-day. Because within-site spread differed between sites, we repeated the test on values ranked within each visit, and we report partial η² for site as the effect size. Pairwise site comparisons used the same test on each pair of sites, with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment within each metric and sample set. With two sites, only the k visits containing both contribute, so all 2^k permutations were enumerated and the smallest attainable P is 2/2^k. Pairs were therefore compared by effect size: the median within-visit difference in diversity, pooled and per subject. The pre-specified primary analysis was Shannon diversity on all samples. Observed ASVs and the analysis excluding the three flagged day-0 palm samples (see Beta diversity) are secondary. Subject-1's right palm samples were all shallow (830–1,125 reads, ID prefix L3), so right palm comparisons involving subject-1 cannot be separated from sequencing depth or run. As further sensitivity analyses, we repeated the calculation at a rarefaction depth of 1,100 reads (excluding the three samples below that depth) and examined within-site Spearman correlations between rarefied diversity and original depth.

**Beta diversity.** We computed Bray-Curtis (abundance-weighted) and Jaccard (presence/absence) distances, each averaged over 100 rarefactions to 830 reads (`vegan::avgdist`), and, as a sensitivity analysis, Bray-Curtis distances on unrarefied relative abundances (`scripts/03_beta_diversity.R`). Distances were ordinated by principal coordinates analysis (classical multidimensional scaling); the share of variation per axis was calculated from the positive eigenvalues, and negative eigenvalues were negligible. Differences between body sites were tested by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; `vegan::adonis2`) with the model distance ~ body site + subject and marginal (type III) sums of squares, using 9,999 permutations. To respect the repeated-measures design, permutations were restricted within visit (subject × day; 9 blocks), so that body-site labels were exchanged only among samples taken from the same person on the same day (`permute::how(blocks = visit)`). The subject term was included to account for between-person differences, and its R² is reported, but it was not tested because two individuals do not permit a valid test. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion between body sites was assessed with `vegan::betadisper` and a permutation test under the same restriction. Pairwise PERMANOVA was performed for each pair of body sites with the same model and permutation scheme, and P values were adjusted with the Benjamini–Hochberg method within each distance measure. Because each visit block contains at most two samples in a pairwise comparison, only 64–512 distinct permutations exist; all were enumerated, and the smallest attainable P value (1/n or, where every block contains one sample of each site, 2/n permutations) is reported alongside each result. Pairwise contrasts are therefore compared by R² rather than by P value.

**Day-0 palm samples.** All three day-0 palm samples clustered with a different body site in the ordination. For each, we identified the nearest samples by Bray-Curtis distance on relative abundance (`results/03_beta_day0_palm_neighbours.csv`). Analysis of all samples was pre-specified as primary. We report two sensitivity analyses: excluding only these three samples (L3S242, L3S378, L2S240), and excluding all seven day-0 samples. The latter was chosen after inspecting the data.

**Taxonomic composition.** Phylum- and genus-level relative abundances were summarised per sample (`scripts/04_taxa_composition.R`). Phyla reaching ≥1% in at least one sample were shown individually; the remainder were grouped as "Other". For each body site, genera were ranked by a subject-balanced mean (the mean of the two subjects' mean relative abundances, so that each person contributed equally), and we report each subject's mean, the median across samples, and the expected prevalence at 830 reads (the probability of detecting the taxon in a subsample of 830 reads, calculated from the hypergeometric distribution). Tables are given for all samples and with the three flagged day-0 palm samples excluded. No differential-abundance testing was performed.

**Gut vs tongue differential abundance.** Genera differing between gut and tongue were identified with ALDEx2 (`scripts/06_genus_gut_vs_tongue.R`), chosen because it treats 16S counts as compositional. ALDEx2 draws 128 Monte Carlo instances of each sample's proportions from a Dirichlet distribution (prior 0.5) and applies a centred log-ratio (CLR, log2) transformation, so that each genus is expressed relative to the geometric mean of all genera in the sample. We used unrarefied genus-level counts (organelles removed; labels as for taxonomic composition) from the 8 visits with both a gut and a tongue sample (4 per subject). Gut and tongue samples were paired by visit. The effect size was ALDEx2's paired effect: the median paired (tongue − gut) CLR difference divided by the median absolute deviation of those differences. This is larger than ALDEx2's usual unpaired effect, so conventional cut-offs (|effect| > 1) do not apply. Genera were classed as shared (detected at both sites) or site-exclusive (0 of 8 samples at the other site; 23 gut-only and 17 tongue-only of 58). For site-exclusive genera, the CLR value at the absent site is set by the prior and the sample's depth, so they were described by detection frequency and mean relative abundance rather than by effect size. Direction consistency was counted over informative visits only (genus detected in at least one sample of the pair), overall, per subject, and with day 0 excluded. Because the two communities are nearly disjoint, the CLR reference (all genera) is dominated by imputed zeros, so the ranking was checked with ALDEx2's `denom = "zero"` and with scale models that shift the tongue total microbial load relative to gut by −4, −2, −1, +1, +2 and +4 log2 units (`aldex.makeScaleMatrix`, γ = 0.5). The interquartile log-ratio reference could not be used because too few genera were present at both sites. ALDEx2 1.38.0's paired Wilcoxon test omits the probability of the observed statistic in one tail, giving p-values that are too small for genera higher in the second group, including impossible values of 0. We therefore computed exact paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with `stats::wilcox.test` on each Monte Carlo instance, applied Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across genera within each instance, and averaged across instances. Because the 8 visits come from two people, these p-values are reported in the output table only, and results are described by effect size and by consistency of direction across visits and subjects.

**Temporal stability.** Using the rarefied Bray-Curtis distances (same settings and seed as above), we calculated the distance between consecutive visits for each subject and body site and recorded the interval between visits, which was 28, 56 or 84 days (`scripts/05_temporal_stability.R`). All 84-day intervals started at day 0, and all 56-day intervals were from subject-2, so interval length is confounded with the first visit and with subject. As benchmarks, we compared (i) all pairs of visits from the same person and site, (ii) the same site in the two subjects on the same day, and (iii) different sites in the same person on the same day, summarising each by its median and range, pooled and per subject. The between-person benchmark compares one pair of individuals measured on 3–4 days, and within-person pairs share samples, so these summaries are descriptive and no tests were performed. To estimate how much distance rarefaction alone introduces, we calculated the Bray-Curtis distance between two independent 830-read rarefactions of the same sample (median of 50 repeats per sample).

All analyses describe these two individuals and are not intended to generalise to other people. Significance, where tested, was assessed at α = 0.05.

## Software

Analyses were performed in R 4.4.3 (R Core Team, 2025), installed with micromamba from conda-forge and Bioconda. We used vegan 2.7.5 (Oksanen et al., 2026) for rarefaction, diversity indices, distances, PERMANOVA and dispersion tests; ALDEx2 1.38.0 (Fernandes et al., 2014) for compositional differential abundance; permute 0.9.10 (Simpson, 2026) for restricted permutations; rhdf5 2.50.0 (Fischer et al., 2024), Matrix 1.7.6 and yaml 2.3.12 to read the QIIME 2 artifact; readr 2.2.0, dplyr 1.2.1, tidyr 1.3.2 and tibble 3.3.1 for data handling; and ggplot2 4.0.3 (Wickham, 2016) with scales 1.4.0 for figures.

## Data and code availability

The 16S data are publicly available from ENA (study ERP021896; BioProject PRJEB19825) and Qiita (study 550). The QIIME 2 tutorial files used here are available from the Moving Pictures tutorial (https://moving-pictures-tutorial.readthedocs.io). All analysis code and outputs are available at https://github.com/JoFrempong/dbbs-workshop; each result can be regenerated by running `Rscript scripts/0N_*.R` from the repository root.
